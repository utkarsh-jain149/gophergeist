The license that killed Gopher

In 1993 the University of Minnesota announced that it would charge license
fees for some uses of its Gopher server software, mostly commercial ones. The
fees were modest, and many people kept using Gopher for free. But the
announcement scared off companies and developers, who no longer knew whether
building on Gopher would cost them.

That same spring, on April 30, 1993, CERN released the World Wide Web software
into the public domain. Anyone could run it, change it, and build on it for
free, with no permission needed. Mosaic, a graphical web browser, arrived
the same year.

People voted with their servers. The web grew explosively and Gopher shrank.
Looking back, the licensing announcement is widely seen as the turning point.
The university eventually released its Gopher server under the GNU GPL in 2000,
but by then the web had won.

The lesson is still with us: a technology that someone can charge you for, or
take away, is a technology people hesitate to build on. Open things spread.
