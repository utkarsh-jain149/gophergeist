The license that killed Gopher

Every ghost story has a curse. This is Gopher's.

In 1993 the University of Minnesota announced that it would charge license
fees for some uses of its Gopher server software, mostly commercial ones. The
fees were modest, and many people kept using Gopher for free. But the
announcement frightened off companies and developers, who no longer knew
whether building on Gopher would one day cost them.

That same spring, on April 30, 1993, CERN released the World Wide Web software
into the public domain. Anyone could run it, change it, and build on it for
free, with no permission needed. Mosaic, a graphical web browser, arrived
the same year.

People voted with their servers. The web grew explosively, and Gopher began
to fade like a voice down a long hallway. Looking back, the licensing
announcement is widely seen as the turning point. The university eventually
released its Gopher server under the GNU GPL in 2000, but by then the web had
won and Gopherspace was mostly empty rooms.

The moral is older than the internet: a thing someone can charge you for, or
take away, is a thing people are afraid to build on. Open things spread.
Closed things are left to haunt.
